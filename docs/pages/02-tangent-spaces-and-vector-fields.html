Tangent Spaces and Vector Fields [S+N]¶

At each point $p$ of a smooth $n$-manifold $M$, the tangent space $T_p M$ is an $n$-dimensional real vector space — the local linear approximation. Three equivalent definitions, each useful:

  1. Velocity vectors: equivalence classes of smooth curves $\gamma : (-\varepsilon, \varepsilon) \to M$ with $\gamma(0) = p$, where $\gamma_1 \sim \gamma_2$ iff they have the same derivative in some (any) chart.
  2. Derivations: linear maps $v : C^\infty(M) \to \mathbb{R}$ satisfying the Leibniz rule $v(fg) = v(f)g(p) + f(p)v(g)$.
  3. Coordinate vectors: in a chart $(U, x^1, \ldots, x^n)$, $T_p M$ is spanned by the partial-derivative operators $\{\partial/\partial x^i|_p\}$.

A vector field $X$ on $M$ is a smooth assignment $p \mapsto X_p \in T_p M$. In local coordinates, $X = X^i\,\partial/\partial x^i$ (Einstein summation).

Tangent vectors as derivations¶

For a function $f : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ and a tangent vector $v$ at point $p$, the directional derivative $v(f) = \nabla f(p) \cdot v$ is the prototypical derivation.

The "derivation" axiomatisation generalises: it works on any manifold, even when there's no global ambient Euclidean space to take gradients in.

Tangent vectors on a surface¶

For a surface $M \subset \mathbb{R}^3$ parametrised by $\mathbf{r}(u, v)$, the tangent space at $\mathbf{r}(u_0, v_0)$ is spanned by the partial derivatives $\mathbf{r}_u, \mathbf{r}_v$:

$$T_p M = \mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{r}_u(u_0, v_0),\; \mathbf{r}_v(u_0, v_0)\}.$$

These are tangent vectors in the ambient $\mathbb{R}^3$, but they form an intrinsic basis of $T_p M$ once you choose coordinates.

For the unit sphere with $\mathbf{r}(u, v) = (\sin v \cos u, \sin v \sin u, \cos v)$ (with $v \in (0, \pi)$ the polar angle, $u \in [0, 2\pi)$ the azimuth), tangent vectors come out:

The pushforward of a smooth map¶

A smooth map $f : M \to N$ has a differential (or pushforward) at each point $p$:

$$df_p \;:\; T_p M \to T_{f(p)} N.$$

In coordinates, $df_p$ is the Jacobian matrix of $f$ written in the local charts. This is the right generalisation of "total derivative" from Stage 2/03 — the Jacobian becomes a coordinate-free linear map between tangent spaces.

The chain rule in this language: $d(g \circ f)_p = dg_{f(p)} \circ df_p$. Beautiful and intrinsic.

Vector fields and integral curves¶

A vector field $X$ on $M$ assigns a tangent vector to each point. Its integral curves are solutions $\gamma(t)$ to the ODE

$$\dot\gamma(t) = X_{\gamma(t)}, \qquad \gamma(0) = p_0.$$

Existence-uniqueness from Stage 2 (specifically, the multivariable version of Picard-Lindelöf) gives us flows $\Phi_t : M \to M$ — the map "follow the field for time $t$". These form a one-parameter group of diffeomorphisms when $X$ is "complete" (e.g., on compact manifolds or with sublinear growth).

Integral curves of vector fields are the abstract setting for autonomous ODE systems.

Lie bracket¶

For two vector fields $X, Y$, the Lie bracket $[X, Y]$ is another vector field. In coordinates,

$$[X, Y]^i = X^j \frac{\partial Y^i}{\partial x^j} - Y^j \frac{\partial X^i}{\partial x^j}.$$

It measures the failure of flows to commute: $[X, Y] = 0$ ⇔ the flows of $X$ and $Y$ commute. For Hamiltonian fields on phase space, $[X_f, X_g] = X_{\{f, g\}}$ where $\{,\}$ is the Poisson bracket — this is the connection between classical and quantum mechanics.

What we got¶

Tangent spaces formalise "infinitesimal direction at a point" without any ambient Euclidean space. Vector fields collect them globally. The pushforward, Lie bracket, and integral curves all live naturally on manifolds.
