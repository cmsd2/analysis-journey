Continuity in $\mathbb{R}^n$¶

A function $f : U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is continuous at $x_0$ if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there's $\delta > 0$ such that

$$\|x - x_0\| < \delta \;\Longrightarrow\; \|f(x) - f(x_0)\| < \varepsilon.$$

Same shape as the 1D definition, but now $\|\cdot\|$ is a vector norm (any of the equivalent ones from the previous notebook).

Equivalently: $f$ is continuous at $x_0$ iff for every sequence $x_k \to x_0$, $f(x_k) \to f(x_0)$. The sequential characterisation is often easiest to use.

The big new phenomenon vs. 1D: limits along different paths into $x_0$ can disagree, so a function with all "directional limits" may still be discontinuous. This is the canonical pitfall of multivariable continuity.

A function with no limit at the origin¶

The textbook example: $f(x, y) = \dfrac{x y}{x^2 + y^2}$ for $(x, y) \ne (0, 0)$.

Approach the origin along the line $y = m x$: $$f(x, mx) = \frac{m x^2}{(1 + m^2) x^2} = \frac{m}{1 + m^2}$$

— independent of $x$! So the limit along each line exists, but it depends on $m$. There's no single value $f$ approaches as $(x, y) \to (0, 0)$, so the function has no limit at the origin (and is therefore not continuous there, regardless of how we extend).

A trickier counter-example¶

Even more pathological: $g(x, y) = \dfrac{x^2 y}{x^4 + y^2}$ at $(0, 0)$.

Along every line $y = mx$ (for any $m$), $g(x, mx) \to 0$ as $x \to 0$.

But along the parabola $y = x^2$: $$g(x, x^2) = \frac{x^2 \cdot x^2}{x^4 + x^4} = \frac{1}{2}.$$

So directional limits all give 0, but a curved path gives $1/2$ — no limit at origin. Linear paths don't capture the subtleties of multivariable limits.

Functions that are continuous¶

Most familiar combinations of continuous functions are continuous:

  • Sums, products, and quotients (where defined) of continuous functions are continuous.
  • Compositions: if $f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ and $g : \mathbb{R}^m \to \mathbb{R}^k$ are continuous, so is $g \circ f$.
  • Component-wise: $f = (f_1, \ldots, f_m)$ is continuous iff every $f_j$ is.

Polynomials, $\sin$, $\exp$ applied component-wise — all fine. The pathologies show up when you divide by something approaching zero.

Continuity on a compact set is uniform — Heine-Cantor¶

A continuous function on a compact set is uniformly continuous. Same conclusion as in 1D, same reason: the set is small enough (compact) that a single $\delta$ works everywhere. This needs no finite-dimensionality — it holds in any metric space (notebook 7).

Consequences (parallel to 1D):

  • Continuous on compact ⇒ bounded, attains max and min (Weierstrass).
  • Continuous on compact ⇒ uniformly continuous.
  • Continuous on compact and connected ⇒ image is compact and connected.

Why "continuous at every point" is often not enough¶

Take the second example, $g(x,y) = x^2 y/(x^4 + y^2)$ (defined to be $0$ at the origin). Its restriction to every line through the origin is continuous: along $y = mx$ it equals $mx/(x^2 + m^2) \to 0 = g(0,0)$. Yet $g$ is not continuous at the origin, because the parabola $y = x^2$ approaches $1/2$.

(The first example, $f = xy/(x^2+y^2)$, fails even this weaker test — its restriction to $y = mx$ is the constant $m/(1+m^2)$ off the origin but $0$ at it, so it is already discontinuous along every line except the axes.)

Continuity is genuinely a multivariable property — every approach has to behave, not just the straight-line ones.

Lipschitz, Hölder, and uniform continuity all generalise to $\mathbb{R}^n$ with the obvious norm modifications. The multidimensional Mean Value Theorem (next notebook) requires more care than the 1D version — there's no single $c$ in higher dimensions, but there's an integral form.
