Vector Calculus and the Stokes Theorems [S+N]¶

Three differential operators on smooth scalar / vector fields in $\mathbb{R}^3$:

  • Gradient $\nabla f = (\partial f/\partial x, \partial f/\partial y, \partial f/\partial z)$ — turns scalars into vector fields.
  • Divergence $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \partial F_x/\partial x + \partial F_y/\partial y + \partial F_z/\partial z$ — turns vector fields into scalars; "outflow per unit volume".
  • Curl $\nabla \times \mathbf{F}$ — turns vector fields into vector fields; "rotation per unit area".

Three integral theorems link these to integrals over boundaries:

  • Green (2D): $\oint_{\partial R} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \iint_R (\partial F_2/\partial x - \partial F_1/\partial y)\,dA$.
  • Stokes (general): $\oint_{\partial S} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \iint_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot d\mathbf{S}$.
  • Divergence: $\iiint_V \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}\,dV = \iint_{\partial V} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S}$.

Each says: integrating a derivative over a region equals integrating the original quantity over the boundary. They generalise the fundamental theorem of calculus to higher dimensions.

Curl in 3D¶

$\nabla \times (-y, x, 0) = (0, 0, 2)$ — pure z-axis rotation, as expected. The full curl formula in 3D is

$$\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \begin{pmatrix} \partial F_z / \partial y - \partial F_y / \partial z \\ \partial F_x / \partial z - \partial F_z / \partial x \\ \partial F_y / \partial x - \partial F_x / \partial y \end{pmatrix}.$$

Verifying Green's theorem [S+N]¶

Green: $\oint_{\partial R} (P\,dx + Q\,dy) = \iint_R \left(\dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \dfrac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right) dA$.

Take $P = -y/2$, $Q = x/2$, $R$ = unit disc. Then $\partial Q/\partial x - \partial P/\partial y = 1$, so the area integral equals $\mathrm{area}(R) = \pi$. The line integral around the unit circle parameterised as $(\cos t, \sin t)$, $t \in [0, 2\pi]$:

$$\oint = \int_0^{2\pi} \left( -\tfrac{1}{2}\sin t \cdot (-\sin t) + \tfrac{1}{2}\cos t \cdot \cos t \right) dt = \int_0^{2\pi} \tfrac{1}{2}\,dt = \pi.$$

Both sides $= \pi$. Maxima confirms.

So $(P, Q) = (-y/2, x/2)$ gives a vector field whose line integral around any closed curve equals the area enclosed. That's a beautiful trick (and the basis of planimeters — mechanical devices that compute area by tracing a boundary).

The divergence theorem [S+N]¶

Divergence theorem: $\iiint_V (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F})\,dV = \iint_{\partial V} \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n}\,dS$.

Take $\mathbf{F}(x, y, z) = (x, y, z)$ and $V$ = unit ball. Then $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = 3$, so the volume integral is $3 \cdot \text{vol}(V) = 3 \cdot 4\pi/3 = 4\pi$. The boundary integral over the unit sphere is $\iint \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n}\,dS = \iint 1 \cdot dS = $ area of sphere = $4\pi$. (On the unit sphere, $\mathbf{F} = (x,y,z)$ equals the outward normal $\mathbf{n}$, so $\mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} = 1$.)

Both sides are $4\pi$. Verify the volume integral with Maxima.

Why the integral theorems unify the subject¶

A single line:

$$\int_M d\omega = \int_{\partial M} \omega.$$

This is the generalised Stokes theorem (in differential-geometric form). It encompasses:

  • The fundamental theorem of calculus ($\int_a^b f' = f(b) - f(a)$),
  • Green's theorem,
  • Stokes' theorem in $\mathbb{R}^3$,
  • The divergence theorem,
  • ...

Gauss-Bonnet is a tempting addition to that list, but it does not belong: it is proved using Stokes together with the structure equations, yet it isn't an instance of $\int_M d\omega = \int_{\partial M} \omega$. A closed surface has no boundary, so if $K\,dA$ were $d\omega$ for some global 1-form $\omega$ the integral would have to vanish — and for $S^2$ it is $4\pi$.

Every one of the theorems above has the same shape: integrate a derivative over a region = integrate the original over the boundary. The differential form $\omega$ and exterior derivative $d$ make this rigorous; what we computed above are the classical incarnations.

What we have at the end of Stage 2¶

Vector calculus closes the multivariable picture:

  • Topology (notebook 1) gave us the right notion of "near" and "compact".
  • Continuity (notebook 2) revealed why limits along all paths matter.
  • Differentiation (notebook 3) replaced the slope with a Jacobian.
  • Inverse/implicit function theorems (notebook 4) said the Jacobian's invertibility controls local behaviour.
  • Multiple integration (notebook 5) generalised the Riemann construction.
  • Vector calculus (this notebook) tied derivatives and integrals back together.

Stage 3 leaves $\mathbb{R}^n$ for $\mathbb{C}$. The two-dimensional structure of $\mathbb{C}$ + a multiplication law is enormously more rigid than $\mathbb{R}^2$ alone — and unlocks tools (residues, conformal maps) without any analogue in real analysis.
