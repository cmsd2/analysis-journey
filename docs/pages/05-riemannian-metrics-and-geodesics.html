Riemannian Metrics and Geodesics [S+N]¶

A Riemannian metric $g$ on a smooth manifold $M$ assigns to each point an inner product on the tangent space $T_p M$, varying smoothly with $p$. In local coordinates,

$$g = g_{ij}(x)\,dx^i \otimes dx^j$$

(symmetric, positive definite at each point). $g$ lets us measure:

  • Lengths of vectors: $\|v\|_g = \sqrt{g_{ij} v^i v^j}$.
  • Angles between vectors via $g(u, v)$.
  • Lengths of curves: $L(\gamma) = \int \|\dot\gamma\|_g\,dt$.

A geodesic is a curve that locally minimises length — the manifold's analogue of a straight line. For $\mathbb{R}^n$ with the standard metric, geodesics are straight lines. On $S^2$, they're great circles. On a general manifold, they solve the geodesic equation:

$$\ddot\gamma^k + \Gamma^k_{ij}\,\dot\gamma^i\,\dot\gamma^j = 0$$

where $\Gamma^k_{ij}$ are the Christoffel symbols of $g$.

Christoffel symbols [S+N]¶

The Christoffel symbols are determined by the metric:

$$\Gamma^k_{ij} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,g^{kl}\,(\partial_i g_{jl} + \partial_j g_{il} - \partial_l g_{ij}).$$

(With Einstein summation; $g^{kl}$ is the inverse metric.) For the round metric on $S^2$ with coordinates $(\theta, \phi)$ ($\theta$ = colatitude, $\phi$ = longitude),

$$g = d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2.$$

Compute the Christoffel symbols symbolically:

coords : [theta, phi]$
gamma_christoffel(k, i, j) := (1/2) * sum(g_inverse[k, l] *

   - diff(g_metric[i, j], coords[l])), l, 1, 2)$

Non-zero Christoffel symbols on S²:
  Γ^ 2 _ 1 2 = cos(theta)/sin(theta)
  Γ^ 2 _ 2 1 = cos(theta)/sin(theta)
  Γ^ 1 _ 2 2 = -(cos(theta)*sin(theta))

On $S^2$, the non-zero Christoffel symbols are:

  • $\Gamma^\theta_{\phi\phi} = -\sin\theta\cos\theta$
  • $\Gamma^\phi_{\theta\phi} = \Gamma^\phi_{\phi\theta} = \cot\theta$.

These are the "fictitious force" terms that appear when you write Newton's laws in non-Cartesian coordinates.

Geodesic equation on $S^2$¶

Plug the Christoffels into the geodesic equation:

$$\ddot\theta = \sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\phi^2,$$ $$\ddot\phi = -2\cot\theta\,\dot\theta\,\dot\phi.$$

Solve numerically with np_cvode to trace a great circle:

Distance and the metric¶

For two points $p, q \in M$, the geodesic distance is

$$d(p, q) = \inf_\gamma L(\gamma)$$

over all smooth paths from $p$ to $q$. This makes $M$ a metric space; for a connected manifold, $d$ defines the same topology as the manifold structure.

For $S^2$ with the round metric, geodesic distance = central angle = $\arccos(\langle p, q\rangle)$ for $p, q$ on the unit sphere.

What metric structure buys¶

With $g$ on $M$, you can:

  • Measure: lengths, angles, areas, volumes.
  • Move: parallel transport (next notebook), geodesic flow.
  • Optimise: minimise functionals like $\int \|\dot\gamma\|$ — calculus of variations.
  • Decompose: orthogonal splittings of vector fields (Helmholtz / Hodge).
  • Quantify how curved the space is (next notebook).

Riemannian geometry is what makes general relativity possible: spacetime is a Lorentzian (signature $(-, +, +, +)$) manifold, geodesics are world-lines of free particles, and the curvature of $g$ encodes gravity.

What's coming¶

The Christoffel symbols hint at what comes next: curvature. Christoffels alone aren't tensors (they transform inhomogeneously under coordinate change), but they combine into a tensor — the Riemann curvature tensor — that encodes how parallel transport fails to commute, how geodesics deviate from each other, and (in 2D) how the manifold differs from being flat.
