Continuity¶

A function $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous at $x_0$ if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is $\delta > 0$ such that

$$|x - x_0| < \delta \;\Longrightarrow\; |f(x) - f(x_0)| < \varepsilon.$$

Read aloud: if you want $f(x)$ within $\varepsilon$ of $f(x_0)$, you can guarantee it by keeping $x$ within $\delta$ of $x_0$. The δ depends on both ε and $x_0$. When the same δ works for every $x_0$, the function is uniformly continuous.

This notebook exposes the difference: pointwise continuity is the weakest useful version, uniform continuity is what you actually need for most theorems (Riemann integrability, the fundamental theorem, exchange of limits...).

A continuous function and its δ-window¶

Take $f(x) = x^2$ at $x_0 = 1$. To guarantee $|f(x) - 1| < \varepsilon$, how small must $|x - 1|$ be?

Algebra: $|x^2 - 1| = |x-1|\,|x+1| < \delta(2 + \delta)$ for $|x-1| < \delta$. To make this $< \varepsilon$, take $\delta = \min(1, \varepsilon/3)$ (sufficient).

Pointwise vs uniform continuity¶

$f(x) = 1/x$ is continuous at every $x \in (0, 1)$ — but not uniformly continuous. As $x_0 \to 0^+$, the slope blows up; the same $\varepsilon$ requires smaller and smaller $\delta$. There's no single $\delta$ that works for every $x_0$.

Quantitative test: at $x_0 = 0.01$, the function value is $100$. To stay within $\varepsilon = 1$, we need $|x - 0.01|$ tiny enough that $1/x$ doesn't change by more than 1. That's $\delta \approx \varepsilon \cdot x_0^2 = 10^{-4}$. As $x_0 \to 0$, the required $\delta$ shrinks to zero.

print("x₀:           ", x0_vals)$
print("δ required (ε=1):", delta_required)$
print("→ δ shrinks to 0 with x₀, so no uniform δ works on (0, 1)")$

On a closed bounded interval $[a, b]$ with $a > 0$, however, $1/x$ is uniformly continuous — by the Heine-Cantor theorem, every continuous function on a compact set is uniformly continuous. The pathology only appears when the interval reaches the singularity.

Lipschitz and Hölder continuity¶

Stronger conditions. $f$ is Lipschitz with constant $L$ if $|f(x) - f(y)| \le L\,|x - y|$ for all $x, y$. Hölder of order $\alpha \in (0, 1]$ is $|f(x) - f(y)| \le C\,|x - y|^\alpha$.

Lipschitz ⇒ uniformly continuous; the converse fails (e.g. $\sqrt{x}$ on $[0, 1]$ is Hölder of order $1/2$ but not Lipschitz, because the derivative blows up at 0).

Pathological: continuous everywhere, nowhere differentiable¶

Weierstrass (1872) constructed $W(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty a^n \cos(b^n \pi x)$ and proved it nowhere differentiable when $0 < a < 1$, $b$ is an odd integer, and $ab > 1 + 3\pi/2 \approx 5.71$. It's continuous either way (uniform convergence of continuous functions; we'll formalise this in 06-uniform-convergence).

Hardy (1916) sharpened the hypothesis to just $0 < a < 1$ and $ab \ge 1$, dropping the parity condition on $b$. The demo below uses $a = 1/2$, $b = 7$, so $ab = 3.5$: enough for Hardy's condition, not enough for Weierstrass's original one.

Truncate to a finite sum to visualise. The graph is rough at every magnification — a fractal.

Why uniform continuity matters¶

Several big theorems require it:

  • Riemann integrability: every continuous function on $[a, b]$ is integrable, because compact ⇒ uniformly continuous (Heine-Cantor).
  • Exchange of limits: $\lim_{n\to\infty} \lim_{x\to x_0} f_n(x) = \lim_{x\to x_0} \lim_{n\to\infty} f_n(x)$ requires uniform convergence (next notebook).
  • Numerical stability: uniformly continuous functions tolerate small input perturbations with bounded error.

Pointwise continuity is enough for evaluating a function, but uniform continuity is what makes analysis hang together.
