Smooth Manifolds¶

A smooth manifold is a space that, locally, looks like $\mathbb{R}^n$. Globally it can curve, twist, wrap around, or have non-trivial topology — but at every point there's a coordinate chart that lets you do calculus as if you were in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

Definition (informal). A smooth $n$-manifold is a topological space $M$ together with an atlas of charts $\{(U_\alpha, \varphi_\alpha)\}$ where:

  1. $U_\alpha \subseteq M$ is open, $\varphi_\alpha : U_\alpha \to \varphi_\alpha(U_\alpha) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is a homeomorphism.
  2. The $U_\alpha$ cover $M$.
  3. Smoothness compatibility: for any two overlapping charts, the transition map $\varphi_\beta \circ \varphi_\alpha^{-1}$ is smooth ($C^\infty$).

The whole point: because transition maps are smooth, "smoothness" is a well-defined notion on $M$, independent of which chart you use.

This notebook walks the canonical examples — circles, spheres, tori — and the smooth-map calculus they support.

The unit circle $S^1$¶

Two charts cover $S^1 = \{(x, y) : x^2 + y^2 = 1\}$:

  • $U_1 = S^1 \setminus \{(0, 1)\}$ (everything except the north pole), $\varphi_1$ = stereographic projection from north pole.
  • $U_2 = S^1 \setminus \{(0, -1)\}$ (everything except the south pole), $\varphi_2$ = stereographic projection from south pole.

The transition map on $U_1 \cap U_2$ is $\varphi_2 \circ \varphi_1^{-1}(t) = 1/t$ — smooth on $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$.

A simpler atlas uses angle: chart $\varphi(\cos\theta, \sin\theta) = \theta$ on $\theta \in (0, 2\pi)$ and another shifted version. But this is just one example.

The 2-sphere $S^2$¶

Same idea: stereographic projection from $(0, 0, 1)$ and $(0, 0, -1)$ gives two charts covering $S^2$. The transition map (in the equatorial annulus) is complex inversion $1/z$ — same flavour as $S^1$.

Why two charts? No single chart can cover all of $S^n$ — a sphere is compact but $\mathbb{R}^n$ isn't, so a continuous bijection between them is impossible. Two charts is the minimum.

The torus $T^2$¶

The product manifold $T^2 = S^1 \times S^1$. Parametrise by two angles $(\theta, \phi)$ with the embedding $(\cos\theta(R + r\cos\phi),\, \sin\theta(R + r\cos\phi),\, r\sin\phi)$ for $R > r > 0$.

Topologically: a doughnut. Locally diffeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^2$ everywhere, but globally has non-trivial loops that can't be contracted (one going around the centre hole, another around the tube).

Smooth maps between manifolds¶

A map $f : M \to N$ between smooth manifolds is smooth if for any charts $(U, \varphi)$ on $M$ and $(V, \psi)$ on $N$ with $f(U) \subseteq V$, the chart representation $\psi \circ f \circ \varphi^{-1}$ is a smooth map of Euclidean spaces.

This is intrinsic: it doesn't depend on the choice of charts, because of the smoothness compatibility of transition maps. A smooth map gives meaning to "differentiable function" without picking coordinates first.

A diffeomorphism is a smooth bijection with smooth inverse. Two manifolds are diffeomorphic if there's a diffeomorphism between them. This is the right notion of "the same manifold" in differential geometry.

Examples of manifolds¶

Manifold Description Dim
$\mathbb{R}^n$ flat Euclidean space $n$
$S^n$ unit sphere in $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ $n$
$T^n = (S^1)^n$ $n$-torus $n$
$\mathbb{RP}^n$ real projective space $n$
$\mathbb{CP}^n$ complex projective space $2n$
$\mathrm{O}(n)$ orthogonal group $n(n-1)/2$
$\mathrm{U}(n)$ unitary group $n^2$
$\mathrm{Gr}(k, n)$ Grassmannian (k-planes in $\mathbb{R}^n$) $k(n - k)$

The whole apparatus of differential geometry applies uniformly to all of them.

Embedded manifolds in $\mathbb{R}^N$¶

Whitney's embedding theorem: every smooth $n$-manifold embeds smoothly in $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$. So abstract manifolds are not strictly more general than embedded ones — but the abstract definition lets us reason without picking an embedding.

In Maxima we'll mostly work with embedded manifolds (parametrised surfaces in $\mathbb{R}^3$, mostly), since they're easy to plot.

What this notebook gives us¶

Manifolds are the right setting for "spaces where calculus works". The next notebooks build up:

  • Tangent vectors as derivations of smooth functions.
  • Vector fields as smooth assignments of tangent vectors.
  • Differential forms as the right things to integrate.
  • Stokes' theorem in its general $\int_M d\omega = \int_{\partial M} \omega$ form.
  • Riemannian metrics and curvature.
  • Lie groups as manifolds with a smooth group structure.

Calculus, freed from the assumption that the underlying space is flat.
